# From Dice to Dugout: The Quiet Math of MiniLeague Baseball

I have a soft spot for baseball games that let me manage instead of making me time a swing. The appeal goes back a long way. Nineteenth-century fans could play baseball at home with boards and simple chance devices. By the 1930s, *National Pastime* was putting individual players' records into the game. Ethan Allen's *All-Star Baseball* turned batting statistics into slices of a spinner; later games such as APBA and Strat-O-Matic gave pitchers and fielders a more substantial say. Personal computers moved the dice and charts behind a screen. The original *MicroLeague Baseball* belonged to that tradition: give me a lineup, a bench, and a few decisions, then let a baseball game unfold. [The National Baseball Hall of Fame](https://baseballhall.org/discover/start-of-fantasy-baseball) and the [Society for American Baseball Research](https://sabr.org/journal/article/how-would-you-like-to-manage-in-the-majors-baseball-board-games-and-their-dedicated-players/) trace that lineage.

*MiniLeague Baseball* feels like a modern answer to the same question: what if I could put two great teams from different years on one field and manage the contest? It offers 15 clubs from 1927 through 1982. I can set the order, choose the starter, call a steal or bunt, position the defense, warm a reliever, or decide that a pitcher has faced one hitter too many. I can also hand either dugout to a computer manager. The animated view has charm, but the part that kept me playing was the moment before a call: a runner on first, one out, a tiring pitcher, and several reasonable ways to be wrong.

## The manager's game

In an action game, skill often means putting a cursor on the ball. Here it means choosing the conditions under which the next play happens. On offense I can swing, send a runner, sacrifice, or try a hit and run. On defense I can change the pitch or alignment, walk a dangerous hitter intentionally, or make a substitution. I have to live with the cost of each choice. Bringing the infield in may stop a run at the plate, but it opens space for a hit. Sending a runner hard may gain a base or lose him to a throw. A reliever needs time to warm, and a pinch hitter spends a player I might need later.

The game advances one plate appearance at a time, with a steal resolved as its own play. It does not simulate every ball and strike. Instead, it estimates pitches thrown so fatigue still matters. That keeps the rhythm brisk: make a baseball decision, see the play, read the new situation, decide again. The Classic overhead field makes the bases easy to follow; the Detailed view gives the result a little theater. The simulation decides what happened first, and the animation stages it afterward, so a close play at home or a catch at the wall is a picture of the recorded result.

## A batting line becomes a set of chances

The engine starts with a batter's actual season totals from the Lahman Baseball Database. Hits, doubles, triples, homers, walks, hit by pitches, and strikeouts become rates per plate appearance. Think of the result as a wheel divided into slices. A home-run hitter gets a larger home-run slice than a light hitter. A patient hitter gets a larger walk slice. One random draw chooses a result from the finished wheel; further draws settle details such as an error, a double play, or whether a runner takes another base.

The important part is that the batter's record is only the starting point. The opposing pitcher changes the slices according to how he performed **relative to pitchers in his own league and year**. A pitcher with more strikeouts than his league average raises the strikeout chance; one who allowed fewer walks lowers the walk chance. This matters when comparing teams decades apart. A 1968 pitcher and a 1927 hitter did their work in different scoring environments. Judging both against one fixed modern average would quietly tilt the whole matchup.

Then the game adjusts for the batter and pitcher's handedness, the park, the fielders' range, the selected pitch, the alignment, and fatigue. Those are probability changes, not scripted outcomes. A strong outfielder makes a hit less likely; he does not catch every ball. A tired pitcher becomes easier to hit; he can still get the next batter out. The left/right adjustment is a general one because the source data does not include each player's split. Fence distances and park factors add flavor, while the game's documentation is candid that its ballpark dimensions are approximate.

This is the statistical idea I appreciate most: *a good decision improves a chance; it does not promise a result.* If I call for a bunt and the batter pops it up, the model has not betrayed me. It has shown me one of the possible prices of the call.

## Five personalities in the other dugout

The computer managers make this more than a solitary exercise in picking the mathematically best button. Ellie Whitcombe, “The Actuary,” prices calls carefully. Rosa Delgado, “Spark,” presses with steals, bunts, and the hit and run. Duke Pruitt, “Big Sky,” lets hitters swing and gives his starter room to work. Marcus Delacroix, “The Tinkerer,” looks for matchup advantages and uses his bench and bullpen readily. Gus Halloran, “Ironhide,” follows an old-school rule book instead of the others' expected-value calculations.

For four of them, the engine asks a practical question: *if I make this call, what do I expect the next situation to be worth?* It uses the same outcome probabilities that will resolve the play. A stolen base can be worth the improved scoring chance, but a caught stealing removes both a runner and an out. A sacrifice can trade a chance at a bigger inning for a better chance to score once. Late in a close game, the managers also consider how a result changes the chance of **winning**, because the first run in a tie game can matter much more than a second insurance run. Each persona sets a different threshold for when an advantage is large enough to justify acting. Halloran's fixed rules offer a different kind of opponent: recognizable baseball instincts, including their occasional stubbornness.

These styles are measurable. In a seeded 6,000-game round robin, Spark attempted steals on 14.9% of eligible chances, while Duke attempted none. The Tinkerer made about 1.75 pitching changes per game; Duke made about 0.80. That does not prove which philosophy is right in every baseball situation. It does show that their advertised habits appear on the field rather than only in their biographies.

## Checking the game against baseball

A simulation can feel convincing and still produce the wrong sport. MiniLeague has two useful checks. One pits the computer personas against one another over thousands of seeded games; it tests whether their styles behave as intended. The other plays the historical teams against one another and compares simulated per-game totals with their real seasons. The two questions are different. A manager can win a tournament played inside an engine that is too hard to hit in.

That distinction caught a substantial problem. An early calibration produced only **62% of the real teams' runs**. The pitcher formula was comparing players with fixed reference numbers even though a batter's season already reflected his era's pitching. In effect, some hitters were being penalized twice. Scaling pitchers against their own leagues, and treating hits and home runs separately from earned-run average, brought the median simulated-to-real run ratio to **0.84**. The calibration notes estimate that about **0.90** is the appropriate target in this particular tournament, because every opponent is a strong historical club rather than an average league opponent.

The latest documented calibration played **8,400 team-games** under historical designated-hitter rules. Median hits were 0.98 of the real totals, doubles 1.00, homers 1.02, and pitchers used per game 1.00. There is still work to do: walks were 0.73 of the reference rate, double plays 0.85, and steal attempts varied too much from team to team even though the overall attempt count was close. Those gaps are useful to know. Calibration is a measuring instrument, not a certificate of perfection.

The test games use fixed random seeds. If a rule changes, the same matchups can be played again and the change in results can be examined. That makes thousands of simulated games more informative than a few memorable innings. It also lets the designers ask a precise question: did a change fix a problem, or did it merely move the numbers around?

After playing, I find myself thinking about the game the way I think about a real manager's night. I remember the bunt that worked, of course, but also the time I left the starter in and watched a small advantage dissolve. MiniLeague gives me quick decisions and a lively field; underneath them is a careful attempt to make the probabilities, the people, and the baseball agree. For a fan of simulation games, that is the pleasure of the whole enterprise.
